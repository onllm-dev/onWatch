Privacy Notice

What onWatch stores on this machine, and everywhere it sends data

In short

onWatch runs on this machine. It polls AI provider quota APIs with your own credentials, writes usage history to a SQLite file on this machine, and serves this dashboard from this machine. The onWatch project receives nothing. There is no analytics, no telemetry, no crash reporting and no onWatch-operated backend of any kind.

That does not make this page unnecessary. onWatch handles account email addresses, OAuth refresh tokens, per-request usage logs and other tools' credential files, and it contacts up to twenty-five third-party endpoints. Everything below is itemised so you can check it against the source.

This page is the same notice that ships as docs/PRIVACY.md in the repository, compiled into the binary you are running. Related controls live in Settings, under General.

Last reviewed 2026-09-16. Describes onWatch v{{.Version}}.

Who is responsible

The onWatch project is not a controller, processor or Data Fiduciary for anything you do with onWatch, for a verifiable reason: no data ever reaches the project. Whoever runs this binary is the Data Controller under the GDPR and the Data Fiduciary under India's DPDP Act 2023.

If you run onWatch on your own machine, for your own accounts, and nobody else can reach this dashboard, you are within the personal and household exemption - GDPR Art. 2(2)(c) and DPDP Act s.3(c)(ii). Data protection law then does not regulate you for it, and this page is documentation rather than an obligation. That exemption stops in three cases:

SituationWhy the exemption stops
The dashboard is reachable by other people - bound to 0.0.0.0, behind a reverse proxy, on a shared LAN, or behind SSOYou are making personal data available to others. The processing is no longer purely personal.
A company, team or client deploys itThe purpose is organisational, not domestic. Full controller and Data Fiduciary duties apply.
It watches an account belonging to someone other than you - an employee's, a contractor'sYou are processing another identifiable person's data, including their AI usage over time. Nothing in onWatch tells that person they are being watched. You have to tell them.

onWatch performs no automated decision-making and no profiling in the sense of GDPR Art. 22. Alerts are fixed numeric-threshold comparisons. There is no advertising, no ad tech, no behavioural tracking, no third-party cookies, and one first-party session cookie only.

How long it is kept

Retention is a policy you set. There are two settings, both a whole number of days, both in Settings under General, and both also settable per deployment with the environment variables ONWATCH_RETENTION_SCRUB_DAYS and ONWATCH_RETENTION_DELETE_DAYS. Both default to zero, which means keep everything forever - that is the default for every install, new and existing. An unset, empty or unparseable value is also read as "keep everything" rather than guessed at, because a retention pass is destructive and an unreadable setting must never be taken as permission to delete. If you need storage limitation, which GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) is not optional about for an organisational deployment, you have to set these yourself.

SettingEffect once a row is older than it
retention_scrub_daysScrub: the identifier and verbatim-payload columns are cleared in place - emails, account names, labels, external_id, project_id, user_id, raw_json, and the telemetry account_name, request_id, metadata_json and source_path. The numeric quota columns beside them are deliberately left alone, so long-term charts survive scrubbing.
retention_delete_daysDelete: the row is removed, with its child rows deleted first so no orphan is left behind. Must be at least retention_scrub_days, or rows would be deleted before they were ever scrubbed. Maximum 36500 days.

The policy is enforced by an hourly pass that re-reads the settings each time, so a change made in the dashboard takes effect within the hour without a restart, and an environment value is the default for whichever of the two periods you have not set in the dashboard. Scrubbing runs before deletion, so a row due for both ends up deleted rather than scrubbed twice. The tables the policy covers come from one declarative registry, and a test fails the build if a table in the schema is left unclassified - that is what stops a newly added provider from quietly creating a table that escapes retention, export and erasure.

Export and erasure

Export. From Settings, or GET /api/privacy/export. Returns one JSON file, onwatch-export-<timestamp>.json, walking every table in the registry rather than only the 17 with obvious identifiers. It is streamed straight out of SQLite so a long history does not blow the 40 MB memory ceiling, and it is capped at 20000 rows per table with any truncation recorded in the output rather than silently dropped. Credential values are replaced with a redaction marker; personal data is not redacted - the point of the file is to show what is held. Treat the download as sensitive: it is a complete copy of the account's usage history.

Erasure. From Settings, or POST /api/privacy/erase, in two scopes: all clears every provider's history plus the device and account records, and provider clears one named provider, which is the proportionate answer when only one account is in question. It requires a typed confirmation, because it cannot be undone.

Two limits to be precise about. Erasure does not shrink the database file - SQLite leaves the freed pages in place, so run VACUUM after a large erasure. That matters most for a database ever written by the original api-integrations build, which holds whole raw telemetry lines in the dropped raw_line column until it is vacuumed. And erasure removes rows, not files.

What erasure cannot reach. A database-side erasure does not remove: the original ~/.onwatch/api-integrations/*.jsonl telemetry events; the debug log and its rotations, with their IP addresses, proxy-asserted usernames and account names (or your container log under Docker); ~/.onwatch/.env; other tools' credential files and the .bak copies onWatch left beside them; ~/.onwatch/quickview-profile/; your backups, which still contain everything you erased from the live database; and the provider's own records - onWatch reads a vendor's API and cannot delete anything on the vendor's side, so an erasure request covering the underlying AI usage has to go to that vendor directly.

Decisions that are the deployer's, not onWatch's: the two retention windows, which default to keeping everything; who can reach this dashboard; whether to enable the telemetry ingest and what your integrations may put in metadata; your backup retention; your container log retention; disk encryption; when to run VACUUM; and whether you tell the people whose accounts you are watching.

Your rights, and the actual mechanism for each

This applies where the GDPR or the DPDP Act applies - in practice to organisational deployments and shared dashboards, not to an individual watching their own accounts at home. onWatch is software, not a service: it provides the mechanism, and the deployer has to receive the request, verify who is asking, act within the statutory deadline and reply.

RightMechanism in onWatch
Access (GDPR Art. 15), portability (Art. 20), information about processing (DPDP s.11)The export - GET /api/privacy/export, from Settings. Machine-readable JSON, every table in the registry. Add the disclosures on this page, which give the purposes, recipients and retention an Art. 15 response also has to state.
Erasure (GDPR Art. 17), DPDP s.12(3), and erasure on consent withdrawal (DPDP s.8(7))The erasure - POST /api/privacy/erase, from Settings, scoped to one provider or to everything, then VACUUM. Read "What erasure cannot reach" above before certifying that erasure is complete - several categories sit outside the database.
Rectification (GDPR Art. 16), correction and completion (DPDP s.12(1))Honestly, it depends on the field. Provider data - emails, account names, plan, project id, usage figures - is fetched read-only from the vendor. onWatch cannot correct it, and if it did the next poll would overwrite the correction. The correction has to be made at the provider, and it then flows in on the next poll, within about two minutes on the default interval. Data you entered locally is directly editable in the dashboard: provider account names, provider tab labels, notification settings, recipients, timezone. If a correction cannot wait for the next poll, delete the affected rows.

Nomination (DPDP Act s.14)

Section 14 lets a data principal nominate someone to exercise their rights on their behalf in the event of death or incapacity. onWatch has no nomination feature - no field for a nominee, no way to verify one, no way to hand a nominee an account. If the DPDP Act applies to your deployment, offering nomination is your duty as Data Fiduciary and has to be handled outside onWatch: accept the nomination through your grievance channel, record it with your other data protection records, verify the nominee when they come forward, and then use export and erasure to give effect to what they ask for.

If you deploy onWatch for a team, decide now, in writing, what happens to a member's usage history when they leave, die or become incapacitated. That is a retention and erasure decision you already have to make.

Children's data (DPDP Act s.9)

onWatch is a developer tool for tracking paid AI provider quotas. It is not directed at children, has no consumer sign-up, no public instance, and no way for anyone to create an account - the deployer sets a single dashboard password. It processes no data it knows or has reason to believe belongs to a child: the only people whose data it holds are the holders of the provider accounts being watched, and those are paid AI accounts held by the deployer.

onWatch cannot verify age and does not try. There is no age gate and no field anywhere that records an age. If a deployer configures onWatch to watch a child's account, onWatch will have no idea, and the s.9(1) verifiable parental consent obligation falls entirely on that deployer.

DPDP s.9(3) is not engaged. That subsection prohibits tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising directed at children. onWatch does none of those things for anyone of any age: no advertising, no ad tech, no third-party trackers, no cross-site identifiers, no profiling, no automated decision-making, and - since every dashboard asset is served from the binary under a 'self'-only policy - no third-party request on page load at all. There is nothing here to exempt, because there is nothing here to prohibit. The same reasoning applies to persons with disabilities who have a lawful guardian under s.9(2).

Cross-border transfers

Relevant under GDPR Art. 44-49 and DPDP Act s.16. onWatch itself transfers nothing anywhere - it has no infrastructure. Every transfer below follows from a destination you chose, under that vendor's own terms, and onWatch has no visibility into it. An EU or UK controller needs a transfer mechanism for each one actually used.

TransferWhere it goes, and what to check
Provider API pollsWherever that vendor processes; most are US companies. Two cases are region-selectable by you, so the jurisdiction is your choice to make and document: Z.ai via api.z.ai versus open.bigmodel.cn (China), and MiniMax via api.minimax.io versus www.minimaxi.com (China). Note also that Moonshot, Kimi and Z.ai are Chinese-headquartered vendors whichever endpoint you use.
OAuth token refresh endpointsconsole.anthropic.com, auth.openai.com, oauth2.googleapis.com, api2.cursor.sh, auth.kimi.com - the same jurisdictions as the vendors.
Your SMTP relayWherever you pointed it. A hosted mail provider may be in another country and may retain message content and metadata under its own policy. Your choice, your transfer, your assessment.

Payloads are encrypted end to end, but the daemon's IP address and the timing of every alert are disclosed. Security safeguards

Not duplicated here, because it would go stale. See SECURITY.md in the repository for the security model, hardening guidance, supported versions and how to report a vulnerability. Three facts from this page most affect your posture, so you do not have to cross-reference for them: the database is not encrypted as a file, and provider_settings, vapid_keys and the SMTP addresses are plaintext inside it, so use full-disk encryption; onWatch writes to other tools' credential files and leaves uncleaned .bak copies of live refresh tokens beside them, which "Auto refresh tokens" turns off; and the dashboard has one shared password, no per-user accounts and no audit trail of who looked at what - if more than one person can reach it, that is the control you are missing, and the debug log's IP addresses are not a substitute.
